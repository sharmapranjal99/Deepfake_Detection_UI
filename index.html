Can deepfake detectors survive the real world?

An interactive guide to the paper “Robustness Analysis of Spatial, Frequency, and Spatial-Frequency Fusion-Based DeepFake Detectors under Adversarial and Real-World Distortions.”
Authors: Rashika, Satyam Tyagi, Mayan Prajapati, Pranjal Sharma, Rachit Gupta.

Aim of the research

Deepfake detectors score well on clean test images. But real images get compressed, resized, blurred, or even deliberately attacked. This paper checks how three kinds of detector hold up when that happens.

How do spatial, frequency, and fusion detectors behave under adversarial attacks and real-world distortions, alone and combined?

What it is

A systematic, side-by-side evaluation of the robustness of spatial, frequency-based, and fusion-based DeepFake detectors under adversarial attacks and real-world distortions.

Dataset

Celeb-DF v2: real and fake celebrity videos. 5 frames are taken from each video.

Main finding

No detector wins everywhere. Adding frequency features to a spatial model does not guarantee better robustness.

Key takeaways

1. Spatial and fusion detectors are strong on clean images and under noise.

2. Tiny FGSM and PGD attacks break all three detectors in the white-box setting.

3. JPEG compression is hard: precision stays high, but recall drops, so many fakes are called real.

4. A high F1-score can be misleading on an imbalanced test set.

Research gap

Earlier work has studied spatial, frequency, and fusion detectors, and separately studied attacks and distortions. But each study used its own datasets, models, and settings, so the results cannot be compared directly.

What exists

Spatial detectorsFrequency (DCT) detectors Fusion detectorsAttack studiesDistortion studies

Most papers build and test one model at a time and show that fusion works.

What is missing

A single common test bench that answers: does fusion actually turn out to be more robust, or does it only add complexity?

How this paper fills it

Same dataset, same splits, same training settings for all three models. Each is tested on clean data, attacks, distortions, and sequences of them.

Novelty: the paper studies how each approach fails under each condition, instead of only reporting clean-image scores. It also checks whether high F1 really means reliable detection.

The three models and how they work

Tap a model to see its working. All three end in a two-output head that gives a Real score and a Fake score, and the higher probability wins.

How the final decision is made

Feature vector>Linear layer → 2 logits> Softmax → probabilities>Higher one wins

Example: Real 0.20, Fake 0.80 → the frame is predicted Fake. During training, cross-entropy loss compares the scores with the true labels and the AdamW optimizer updates the weights.

Attacks and distortions tested

Adversarial attacks

FGSM adds one small step in the direction that raises the model’s loss.

PGD repeats small steps and keeps the change within a limit, so it is stronger.

FGSM ε = 0.001, 0.003, 0.005PGD ε = 0.003, 0.005 PGD: 10 steps, α = 0.001

Real-world distortions

JPEG quality 60Gaussian noise σ = 2 Gaussian blur σ = 2Resize scale 0.5

Compound tests apply these in order, for example PGD → JPEG → Blur → Resize.


“We selected these parameter values as fixed experimental settings to simulate different types of image degradation. JPEG quality 60 represents lossy compression, Gaussian noise and blur simulate noisy or unclear images, and a resize scale of 0.5 simulates reduced resolution."

How performance is measured

Fake is the positive class and the threshold is 0.5.
Precision: of the frames called fake, how many really are.
Recall: of the real fakes, how many were caught.
F1 combines both.
ROC-AUC shows how well real and fake are separated at any threshold.
Balanced accuracy = (recall + specificity) / 2, which treats both classes equally.

Results

The spatial and fusion models demonstrate strong performance on clean and noisy images. However, FGSM and PGD adversarial attacks reduce the performance of all three detectors. JPEG compression and compound distortions further affect detection accuracy, highlighting the challenges of maintaining robustness under multiple image degradations. The frequency-based model performs comparatively weaker in most tested conditions, while the fusion model does not consistently outperform the spatial model. Overall, the comparison shows that no single model performs best across every condition, and combining spatial and frequency features does not automatically guarantee better robustness.

Future work: repeat with several random seeds, try other datasets, and test different distortion strengths.


“Our key finding is that high performance on clean images does not necessarily translate into robustness under real-world distortions and adversarial attacks. Reliable DeepFake detection therefore requires evaluating not only how accurately a model detects fakes, but also how consistently it performs under challenging conditions.”
